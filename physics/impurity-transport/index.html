    Impurity Transport

    Flan follows impurity ions through a background plasma (usually from Gkeyll) by integrating each particle's full orbit, including its gyromotion. No guiding-center approximation is made. Each particle is pushed through the background electric and magnetic fields with the Boris algorithm, and its charge state and velocity are updated between pushes by ionization/recombination and Coulomb collisions.

    The main loop

    Particles are held in a fixed number of slots. Each pass through the main loop (ImpurityTransport namespace, impurity_transport.cpp) advances every alive particle in the slots by one time step, imp_time_step:

    1. Ionization/recombination (if imp_iz_recomb = "on"): the charge state may change based on OpenADAS rate coefficients. See Ionization/Recombination.
    2. Collisions (if imp_collisions = "nanbu"): the velocity is scattered by the background ions and electrons with the Nanbu model. See Collisions.
    3. Boris push: the velocity is updated from the Lorentz force (described below).
    4. Step: the position is moved forward by \(\mathbf{v}\,\Delta t\), and the particle's time is advanced.
    5. Bounds check: particles that leave the domain are absorbed, wrapped (periodic boundaries) or otherwise handled by the boundary conditions.
    6. Fill slots: the slots of dead particles are filled with newly launched particles until none are left to follow.
    7. Find cell: the grid cell and background time frame that contain each particle are found.
    8. Record statistics: each particle's contribution to the impurity density and other outputs is added to the cell it's in.

    The loop runs until every slot is dead and there are no particles left to launch. A particle is also killed once it has been followed for longer than imp_max_time.

    Each step has a CPU (OpenMP) and GPU (CUDA) implementation. A wrapper function picks between them based on use_gpu, so the physics is the same either way.

    Coordinates

    The background is defined on a computational grid \((x, y, z)\) that is generally curvilinear, e.g., field-aligned coordinates. The Lorentz force is much simpler to evaluate in Cartesian space, though, so each particle carries two velocities:

    • \((v_X, v_Y, v_Z)\): the Cartesian velocity. This is what the Boris algorithm updates.
    • \((v_x, v_y, v_z)\): the velocity in computational coordinates. This is what moves the particle on the grid.

    After the Cartesian velocity has been updated, the computational velocity is found with the reciprocal (contravariant) basis vectors \(\nabla x\), \(\nabla y\), \(\nabla z\):

    \[ v_x = \frac{\partial x}{\partial X} v_X + \frac{\partial x}{\partial Y} v_Y + \frac{\partial x}{\partial Z} v_Z \]

    and similarly for \(v_y\) and \(v_z\). The basis vectors are stored at the cell centers, and are trilinearly interpolated to the particle's position.

    The Boris algorithm

    The Boris algorithm is the standard method for pushing charged particles through electric and magnetic fields. It is second-order accurate and time-reversible. A pure magnetic field does no work, and the algorithm conserves energy exactly in that case, so the gyro-orbit doesn't drift over the many gyro-periods a particle is followed for.

    Leapfrog staggering

    Positions and velocities are staggered by half a time step (leapfrog):

    \[ \mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{x}(t_n), \qquad \mathbf{v}_n = \mathbf{v}(t_n - \Delta t/2) \]

    Particles are launched with the position and velocity at the same time, so the velocity is pushed backwards by half a time step (\(-\Delta t/2\)) before the main loop starts. This sets up the staggering.

    Field interpolation

    Before the push, \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are found at the particle's position and time:

    1. Each Cartesian component, and the magnitude, is trilinearly interpolated in space between the centers of the cell containing the particle and its neighbors on the particle's side. This is done for the particle's nearest background time frame and the neighboring frame on the particle's side in time, so the particle's time is always between the two frames.
    2. The two values are linearly interpolated in time to the particle's time.
    3. The interpolated vector is rescaled to the interpolated magnitude.

    Interpolation keeps the fields continuous as a particle crosses cell boundaries and background time frames. With piecewise-constant fields, particles get unphysical "kicks" at every boundary and drift off the field line (numerical diffusion). Interpolating in time also matters for time-dependent drifts like the polarization drift.

    The rescaling in step 3 is a deliberate trade-off. An interpolated vector is almost always shorter than the true field, which would make the gyroradius wrong. Rescaling to the interpolated magnitude gets the gyro-orbit right, at the cost of a small discontinuity at cell boundaries. A particle spends far more time inside a cell than crossing a boundary, so getting the orbit right matters more.

    The push

    With the charge-to-mass ratio \(q/m\) (where \(q = Z e\) for charge state \(Z\)), the velocity is advanced from \(\mathbf{v}_n\) to \(\mathbf{v}_{n+1}\):

    1. Half electric kick:

      \[ \mathbf{v}^- = \mathbf{v}_n + \frac{q}{m}\mathbf{E}\,\frac{\Delta t}{2} \]

    2. Magnetic rotation. With

      \[ \mathbf{t} = \frac{q}{m}\mathbf{B}\,\frac{\Delta t}{2}, \qquad \mathbf{s} = \frac{2\mathbf{t}}{1 + |\mathbf{t}|^2} \]

      the velocity is rotated about \(\mathbf{B}\) in two steps:

      \[ \mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v}^- + \mathbf{v}^- \times \mathbf{t}, \qquad \mathbf{v}^+ = \mathbf{v}^- + \mathbf{v}' \times \mathbf{s} \]

    3. Second half electric kick:

      \[ \mathbf{v}_{n+1} = \mathbf{v}^+ + \frac{q}{m}\mathbf{E}\,\frac{\Delta t}{2} \]

    The rotation leaves \(|\mathbf{v}^+| = |\mathbf{v}^-|\), so all energy change comes from the electric field. Finally, \((v_x, v_y, v_z)\) are calculated from the new Cartesian velocity (see Coordinates), and the step moves the particle:

    \[ \mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n + \mathbf{v}_{n+1}\,\Delta t \]

    Implementation

    The CPU version is Boris::update_velocity_cpu in src/boris.cpp, parallelized over particles with OpenMP. The GPU version is update_velocity_kernel in src/cuda/boris.cu, with one thread per particle. The two use the same push. The GPU version builds the interpolation stencils with the helpers in include/cuda/interpolate.cuh. The core of the push is:

    // Charge state times the (positive) elementary charge, divided by mass
double q_m {slots.q()[i] * -Constants::charge_e / slots.mass()};

// t vector
for (int j {}; j < 3; ++j) t[j] = q_m * B_local[j] * 0.5 * dt;
double tmag2 {t[0]*t[0] + t[1]*t[1] + t[2]*t[2]};

// s vector
for (int j {}; j < 3; ++j) s[j] = 2 * t[j] / (1.0 + tmag2);

// Half electric kick
for (int j {}; j < 3; ++j) vminus[j] = v[j] + q_m * E_local[j] * 0.5 * dt;

// Magnetic rotation
vminus_cross_t = Utilities::cross_product(vminus, t);
for (int j {}; j < 3; ++j) vprime[j] = vminus[j] + vminus_cross_t[j];
vprime_cross_s = Utilities::cross_product(vprime, s);
for (int j {}; j < 3; ++j) vplus[j] = vminus[j] + vprime_cross_s[j];

// Second half electric kick
slots.set_vX(i, vplus[0] + q_m * E_local[0] * 0.5 * dt);
slots.set_vY(i, vplus[1] + q_m * E_local[1] * 0.5 * dt);
slots.set_vZ(i, vplus[2] + q_m * E_local[2] * 0.5 * dt);

    Constants::charge_e is the electron charge (\(-1.602\times10^{-19}\) C), so q * -charge_e is the positive ion charge \(Ze\).

    Neutrals

    Neutrals are handled correctly

    Particles in charge state \(Z = 0\) are followed in the same loop as the ions and need no special treatment in the Boris push. With \(q = 0\):

    • \(q/m = 0\), so both electric kicks vanish,
    • \(\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{s} = 0\), so there is no magnetic rotation,

    and \(\mathbf{v}_{n+1} = \mathbf{v}_n\). A neutral moves in a straight line at constant velocity, as it should, and it isn't affected by the fields.

    The other steps also treat neutrals correctly:

    • Collisions: neutrals are skipped, since they don't undergo Coulomb collisions.
    • Ionization/recombination: a neutral can ionize to \(Z = 1\) but can't recombine, so its recombination probability is zero. Once it's ionized, the next Boris push uses the new charge, and the particle starts to gyrate.

    A neutral's velocity can only change when it ionizes. Until then, it just streams through the plasma.
